The first book
of Norin.

A compact and phonetic way to write English, with a little bit of magic.

In my teens, I was obsessed with codes. Little secret messages that only you and your friends could read. I would spend hours inventing little micro-languages that would survive for days or weeks, until I made new ones.

As I moved into my early twenties, that passion turned into a love of language. Not any language in particular, really, just languages as a whole. I studied Japanese, German, Italian, French, Spanish, and a bunch of others to varying degrees. Among them, Japanese was my favorite.

Japanese kana has this sort of predictability that makes reading and learning feel consistent. Being able to look at a word written in kana and know how to pronounce it, along with how pleasing it is to look at, was such an interesting concept to me as a native English speaker.

It was around that point that I started messing around with conlangs (constructed languages). Conlangs were particularly interesting to me: whole languages and writing systems created for different purposes. Obviously, I tried to make a few myself, but none of them ever reached a point I was happy with.

Eventually, I started on a language called Senarseha and an accompanying writing system called RunikBlu. RunikBlu was heavily inspired by Japanese and Nordic runes, and I quickly fell in love with it. Over time, however, Senarseha and RunikBlu ended up forgotten like the others.

Years later, I rediscovered RunikBlu and wanted to give it the love it deserved. So I remade it from scratch and created Norin.

Norin is a (mostly)phonetic writing system for English. I wanted it to inspire that same sense of wonder I got from learning languages, while also having some practical uses, like being dyslexia-friendly and compact. It brings together my love of languages, fantasy, and secret messages, while still being easy to learn and read.

While it’s intended to be used with phonetic spelling and grammar adjustments for English, it can also be used without changing the grammar. It can even be used for some other languages, to varying degrees.

Norin is my oldest passion project, and I’m glad to be able to share it with you.

I’ll start with the signs, then walk you through spelling and reading sentences. English and Norin examples sit side by side so you can check your reading as you go. You don’t need to memorize the whole alphabet before trying your first words.

The signsChapter 01
Start with recognition

Meet the alphabet.

Norin is a way to write English. You’re learning new signs and spelling conventions, but you should speak the sentences as English. The characters represent sounds, including common English sounds such as sh and ee that English writes with more than one letter. Read from left to right.

The five vowels

I suggest learning the full vowel signs first. Once those look familiar, try their accent forms: a vowel written as a small mark on the preceding sign.

Reading vowel accents

The vowel characters above are shown on their own so you can learn their shapes. In a word, A, E, I, O, and U normally become accents on the character before them. A vowel at the start of a word keeps its full shape. Read the character first, then its accent.

Here’s the same B character with each of the five vowel accents. The labels underneath show the reading order. These are short character exercises, so there’s no spelling conversion to work out.

At the start, or after a character?

at · A starts the word
bat · A follows B

at: full A → T.   bat: B with an A accent → T.

In at, there’s no character before the A, so I write the full A. In bat, the A sits on the B as an accent. You still read b-a-t; the vowel hasn’t disappeared, it’s just taking up less space.

When one vowel follows another

If a vowel follows another vowel, write the second one as a full character. It can then take its own position in a block. You don’t put two vowel accents on the same character.

Take boat. The O becomes an accent on B. The A follows that O, so the A is a full character. That leaves three characters to arrange into a block: B with its O accent, then A, then T.

boat · unstacked
boat · one block

Position 1: B with an O accent.   Position 2: full A, fitted into the block.   Position 3: T.

This applies to the five vowels A, E, I, O, and U. Characters such as EE and OO represent their own sounds and don’t have vowel-accent forms. For example, we is written with W and the EE character, not W and an E accent.

Eight common sounds

These are new characters for common sounds in English. I use familiar English spellings to label them here, but each is a character in its own right.

The remaining letters

These are the 18 consonant and consonant-like signs, including y. There are no separate base signs for c, q, or x; the spelling conventions normally use other characters for their sounds, but you can write those letters normally if you're not using our grammar.

How a block is built

I use sinogram blocks to fit more writing into less space. Their arrangement is inspired by Korean Hangul. You always read the signs in the same order, so a compact block can still be read one sign at a time.

  1. Recognize the base signs and any vowel accents.
  2. A block holds three characters: one full-size character on the left, then two smaller characters on the upper and lower right. Read them in that order.
  3. One or two signs left over use their full forms. Spaces and punctuation separate runs.

Accents inside a block

An accent stays with its character when that character moves into a block. It doesn’t take up one of the three reading positions. Here’s flat both ways:

flat · unstacked
flat · one block

Position 1: F.   Position 2: L with an A accent.   Position 3: T.

Read the left character as F, the upper-right character and its accent as LA, then the lower-right character as T. The result is still flat. Once you recognize an accent, try reading it together with the character carrying it.

The conventionsChapter 02
Keep the mouth in mind

Write the shape of the word.

I base Norin spelling on how a word sounds, how your mouth forms it, and which letters help you recognize it. “Mostly phonetic” matters here: I don’t remove every unvoiced letter. If you still mouth a letter, or it provides useful context, it can stay.

Convention English → working spelling
Keep useful mouthed letters. hope → hope
boat → boat
comb → komb
Use established word exceptions. write → rit
have → hav
said → sed
does → duz
Use the appropriate consonant. cat → kat
quick → kwik
phone → fone
Soften G in known words, not every word. giant → jiant
magic → majik
give → give
Shorten common doubled letters. hello → helo
happy → hapi
Use I for a final vowel Y, including before plural S. boy → boi
boys → bois
yes → yes

I use “working spelling” for the Latin-letter step between English and Norin signs. For example, write → rit shows you which signs to choose before you arrange them into a block. Say the word, notice how your mouth forms it, then choose the characters you need. The working spelling helps you keep track while you’re learning; you don’t need to write it out every time.

Grammar stays familiar.

Keep the grammar you already use in English: word order, verb tenses, questions, and punctuation all stay familiar. I’m changing how the words are written, so you can focus on reading the signs without learning a new sentence structure.

  • Statements: keep the subject, verb, and remaining words in their English order.
  • Questions: keep English question order and the question mark.
  • Endings: retain grammatical endings; their written letters may change under the spelling rules.
  • A special convention: I change the whole word and to y. This is a reference to spanish, and also why the symbol for y looks like a short-hand & sign.
The methodChapter 03
Three passes

From a sentence to signs.

  1. Start with the English. Preserve its meaning, word order, and punctuation.
  2. Apply the spelling conventions. Work out which sounds the word uses and apply the word exceptions. Keep useful mouthed letters in mind, too.
  3. Read the signs. Choose the characters for those sounds, attach eligible vowel accents, then form the three-sign blocks.

Write your first sentence.

Try We can help. on paper. Start with the working spelling, wee kan help. Choose the characters, add the vowel accents, then arrange the characters into blocks. Keep a clear space between words.

  1. Work one word at a time. A vowel at the start of a word stands on its own. A vowel after a non-vowel character is written as an accent. A vowel following another vowel stays full.
  2. Build the blocks in reading order. Write the left character first, then the upper right, then the lower right. Keep each accent with the character it belongs to.
  3. Read your sentence back. Follow the same path you used to write it. Check that the words are separated and the accents are clear.

Copy the example once, then cover it and try again. After that, change a word or write a short sentence of your own.

Use familiar punctuation and leave enough room between words that you can find where each one starts. You’re aiming for writing you can comfortably read back, not a perfect copy of the font.

The reading roomChapter 04
Read first. Reveal second.

A little practice, every day.

Start by finding the spaces between words. Read the full sign and its vowel accent, if it has one, then move through the top and bottom signs. Try saying the sentence before opening the answer. If you get stuck, check one word and have another go.

Write your own line

Pick a few signs you recognize, then try a short sentence of your own. Write it on paper, then read it back one word at a time. Use the alphabet and worked examples if you need a reminder. You can learn the shape of a whole word and still come back to its individual signs whenever you need to.

Keep this page closeChapter 05
Pocket reference

The essentials, at a glance.

  • 31 base signs: five vowel characters, eight characters for additional common sounds, and 18 other characters.
  • Read left to right: left character → upper-right character → lower-right character within a block.
  • Vowel accents: read the accent after the sign carrying it.
  • Spelling: preserve useful mouth shape and context, not just voiced sounds.
  • Grammar: keep the English sentence structure.
  • Check your writing: read each character and its accent in order, then read the whole word.

Try a little at a time: recognize five signs, read three words, then read one sentence without opening the answer. Come back to the alphabet whenever you need it.
